Aerodynamics
The four forces diagram shows an airplane with an arrow pointing in each direction: thrust pulling forward out of the propeller, drag pulling back behind the tail, lift pointing straight up from the wing, and weight pointing straight down.
a. Four Forces
  - Thrust – forward force produced by the powerplant/propeller. It opposes the force of drag. It acts parallel to the longitudinal axis.
  - Drag – rearward force (retarding force) caused by disruption of airflow by the aircraft structure. It opposes the force of thrust. It acts parallel and rearward to the relative wind.
  - Weight – It pulls the aircraft downward due to gravity. Combined load of the aircraft, crew, fuel, and cargo. It opposes the force of lift. It acts vertically downward through the aircrafts center of gravity (CG)
  - Lift – Upward force produced by the dynamic effect of air acting on the wing. It opposes the force of weight. It acts perpendicular to the flight path and through the wings center of lift (CL)
b. Load Factor – ratio of lift to the total weight of the aircraft.
c. Left Turning Tendencies
  - P-Factor
    - Descending propeller blade produces more thrust than the ascending blade. Creating asymmetrical thrust across the propeller.
  - Spiraling Slipstream
    - Rotation of the propeller sends air in a spiraling rotation toward the rear of the aircraft, striking the vertical stabilizer on the left, pushing the nose of the aircraft left.
  - Torque Reaction
    - Involves Newton's Third Law, as the internal parts of the engine revolve in one direction, an equal and opposite force tries to rotate the aircraft in the opposite direction.
d. Drag
  - Induced – the byproduct of lift, varies inversely with airspeed.
  - Parasite – caused by the friction of air moving over the aircraft structure, varies directly with airspeed.
    - Form Drag – created by the shape of the aircraft or component
    - Interference Drag – created by the collision of different airstreams creating turbulence or restricts smooth airflow
    - Skin Friction Drag – created between air molecules and the surface of the aircraft

The airfoil diagram shows a wing cross-section with its rounded leading edge facing the oncoming air and its thin trailing edge at the back. The curved top is the upper surface and the flatter bottom is the lower surface. The chord line is drawn straight through the airfoil from the trailing edge to the leading edge, and the camber line curves through the middle, halfway between the upper and lower surfaces. The relative wind arrives head-on toward the leading edge, and the angle of attack is the angle between the chord line and the relative wind.
- Leading Edge – the part of the airfoil that meets the relative wind first
- Trailing Edge – the part of the airfoil where the airflow of the upper surface and lower surface rejoin
- Chord Line – the imaginary straight line drawn through the airfoil from the trailing edge to the leading edge
- Camber Line – the imaginary line that is equidistant at all points between the upper and lower surfaces

Bernoulli's Principle
The Bernoulli diagram shows air flowing around an airfoil, splitting at the front and passing over and under the wing. The air over the curved top has the longer travel distance, so it is faster moving air with less pressure. The air along the bottom has the shorter travel distance, so it is slower moving air with more pressure. That pressure difference, higher below and lower above, is drawn as lift arrows pointing up from the top of the wing.
- Bernoulli's Principle – as the velocity of a fluid increases, the pressure and temperature decreases
- Newton's 3rd Law of Motion – for every action there is an equal and opposite reaction
