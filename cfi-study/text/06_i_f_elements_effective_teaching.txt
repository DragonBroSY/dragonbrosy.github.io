Teaching risk identification, assessment, and mitigation (AIH 1-2)

What is “risk”?
Risk is the probability and possible severity of accident or loss from exposure to various hazards, including injury to people and loss of resources.

What are the principles of risk management?
• Accept no unnecessary risk
• Make risk decisions at the appropriate level
• Accept risk when the benefits outweigh the cost
• Integrate risk management into planning at all levels

What is the 3-Step process to risk management?
Step 1: Identify the Hazard
A hazard is any actual or potential condition that can lead to degradation, injury, illness, death, or damage to equipment or property. Identifying risks involves using experience, common sense, and specific analytical tools. If a pilot determines that a hazard may pose a risk to the flight, it should be further analyzed.
Step 2: Assess the Risk
Each identified risk is evaluated based on its likelihood (probability) and the severity (consequences) resulting from the hazards, considering the exposure of humans or equipment. A comprehensive risk assessment is then conducted, often using a risk assessment matrix, such as an online Flight Risk Awareness Tool (FRAT), to determine the probability and severity of an accident.
Step 3: Mitigate the Risk
Explore specific strategies and tools to reduce, mitigate, or eliminate the risk. Actions may be taken to lower the likelihood and/or severity of high and serious risks. Medium and low risks typically do not require mitigation. Effective control measures focus on reducing or eliminating the most critical risks. The analysis should also consider the overall costs and benefits of these actions, offering alternative options when possible.

Teaching risk management tools - Pilot/Aircraft/enVironment/External Pressures (PAVE) checklist (AIH 1-4)

Describe the components of the Pilot/Aircraft/enVironment/External Pressures (PAVE) checklist used in aviation risk assessment. How does each component contribute to ensuring flight safety? Provide examples for each component to illustrate its significance in the pre-flight planning process.
The PAVE checklist is a systematic risk assessment tool utilized in aviation to evaluate various factors that could affect the safety of a flight. It consists of four main components:
Pilot in Command:
This component assesses the readiness and qualifications of the pilot. It includes factors such as the pilot's experience, currency, physical and mental fitness, and any personal factors that may impact their ability to operate the aircraft safely. For example, a pilot who is fatigued or stressed may be more susceptible to errors during flight operations.
Aircraft:
This component focuses on the condition and suitability of the aircraft for the intended flight. It involves evaluating factors such as aircraft maintenance status, performance capabilities, equipment functionality, and weight and balance calculations. For instance, if the aircraft has unresolved maintenance issues or is operating beyond its weight limits, it could compromise flight safety.
EnVironment:
This component examines environmental factors that could pose risks to the flight. It includes considerations such as weather conditions (current and forecasted), terrain and obstacles along the route, airspace restrictions, and airport conditions. For example, flying in adverse weather conditions or through mountainous terrain without adequate navigation aids could increase the risk of accidents.
External Pressures:
This component addresses external influences or pressures that may affect decision-making during flight operations. It encompasses factors such as time pressures, passenger expectations, organizational policies, and social pressures. An example of external pressure is the desire to complete a flight on schedule, which may lead pilots to overlook safety concerns or push the limits of aircraft performance.

Teaching risk management tools - Flight Risk Assessment Tools (FRATs) (AIH 1-10)

How do FRATs contribute to flight safety, and what factors are typically assessed using these tools?
Flight Risk Assessment Tools (FRATs) are systematic methods utilized in aviation to enhance flight safety by identifying hazards and enabling pilots and operators to make informed decisions to minimize or mitigate those risks. A FRAT uses the likelihood and severity of the event to quantify risk.

When and how to introduce risk management (AIH 10-2)

When and how should risk management be incorporated into flight training as an aviation instructor?
Risk management should be introduced at the very beginning of flight training and integrated into all aspects of actual flight training, rather than being taught as a separate subject. Ground instruction on risk management should be provided before the first flight lesson and included in formal ground school. Risk management discussions should be a part of all preflight and postflight briefings, with learners encouraged to participate and eventually lead these discussions as they gain more experience.
Furthermore, risk management training should not be limited to initial training but should also be included in recurrent training, transition training, flight reviews, instrument proficiency checks, and other training and currency events. By doing so, instructors can ensure that learners continuously develop and apply risk management skills throughout their aviation careers.

Risk management teaching techniques by phase of instruction (AIH 10-4)

How should aviation instructors teach risk management techniques throughout different phases of instruction?
Aviation instructors should teach risk management using a building block approach, effective for both new and experienced pilots.
Risk Management Training through the Private Pilot Level:
Before First Flight:
Introduce risk management before the first flight and make it a routine throughout initial training. Emphasize practical techniques and skills to comply with the Airman Certification Standards (ACS).
Pre-Solo:
During pre-solo instruction, include risk management in every preflight and postflight brief. Introduce the learner to a non-numerical Flight Risk Assessment Tool (FRAT) and demonstrate its use. By the first solo, learners should conduct basic risk management analysis.
Post-Solo Prior to Cross-Country Training:
After the initial solo flights, learners should perform risk analyses with occasional instructor coaching. Instructors should review the risk analysis for all solo flights and provide feedback. Learners should debrief the instructor on risk management aspects after each solo flight.
Cross-Country Training:
During cross-country training, learners should master risk management techniques suited to complex flights and varied terrains, including night flights, complex airspace, and unfamiliar airports. Learners should complete a full risk analysis for every dual and solo cross-country flight using a FRAT or other method. Instructors should review and approve these analyses as part of the preflight preparation. By following these structured phases, instructors can ensure learners develop and apply effective risk management skills throughout their training.

Managing risk during flight instruction - Common flight instruction risks (AIH 10-6)

Identify and explain the common risks associated with flight instruction. How can instructors effectively mitigate these risks during both ground and flight training sessions?
These involve qualification and aeromedical risks. Instructors must be more proficient and familiar with the aircraft, avionics, and procedures than the learners. Both the instructor's and learner's aeromedical conditions should be monitored. Instructors should be prepared for learner mistakes and plan activities that allow sufficient time and space for safe practice and instructor intervention.
Aircraft Risks:
Instructors may not always be aware of the maintenance status of aircraft used for training, which can lead to hazards from inoperative systems or overdue inspections. Limited payload in trainer aircraft and performance issues in high-density altitudes also pose risks.
Environmental Risks:
Training airspace can be crowded, increasing collision hazards, especially in areas with restricted visibility. Complex and restricted airspace, practicing maneuvers like full stalls or simulated engine failures, and conducting practice approaches without ATC can create additional risks.
External Pressure Risks:
Learners may face scheduling conflicts, aircraft issues, weather problems, and other external pressures like work or family concerns, leading to distractions and anxiety that degrade performance.
Mitigation Strategies:
Instructors should conduct thorough risk assessments before instruction and involve learners in the risk management process. This includes emphasizing shared responsibility for situational awareness and instructing learners on aircraft airworthiness and environmental factors.
Specific mitigations include:
• Ensuring instructor familiarity with aircraft and procedures.
• Monitoring both instructor and learner aeromedical conditions.
• Checking aircraft maintenance status and addressing any issues before flight.
• Avoiding crowded or complex airspace for training.
• Adapting lesson plans based on weather and other environmental conditions.
• Allowing flexible scheduling to reduce learner stress and distractions.
By following these practices, instructors can effectively manage risks and ensure a safer training environment.

Managing risk during flight instruction - Best practices (AIH 10-7)

What are the best practices for managing risk during flight instruction?
Flight instructors can effectively manage risk by following outlined risk management procedures and involving learners in the process. This includes emphasizing shared responsibility for maintaining situational awareness, like lookout for other air traffic, and teaching learners to help with aircraft airworthiness and understanding the training environment, such as airspace and NOTAMs.
Specific mitigations include:
Pilot Risks:
Ensure instructors are qualified, familiarizing themselves with aircraft models and avionics through manuals or flight time. Monitor aeromedical risks using the IMSAFE model and encourage learners to disclose their own aeromedical issues to reschedule if necessary.
Aircraft Risks:
Verify the aircraft's airworthiness status before flights and involve learners in this process to highlight risk management practices. Resolve any discrepancies with maintenance personnel before the preflight inspection.
Environmental Risks:
Address risks from weather, terrain, night operations, and other environmental factors by assessing and mitigating them accurately. Involve learners in risk assessments and mitigations, such as modifying lesson plans or using flight simulators when conditions are not ideal.
External Pressure Risks:
Be aware of learners' schedule limitations and external pressures, emphasizing flexibility in scheduling and lesson plans. Be willing to change training from flight to classroom or terminate a lesson early if external pressures affect learner performance.
By integrating these practices, instructors can manage risks effectively and ensure a safe learning environment.

Managing risk during flight instruction - Special considerations while teaching takeoffs and landings (AIH 10-8)

What are the special considerations that flight instructors should keep in mind while teaching takeoffs and landings?
Takeoffs:
During takeoffs and landings, flight instructors should remember that the time for an aircraft to begin takeoff and initiate a climb is only a matter of seconds, making it difficult to teach effectively during this phase. The learner's attention is primarily focused on maneuvering the aircraft safely, so instructions given during takeoff may not be processed. Therefore, most teaching should be done before contacting the tower or announcing intentions on the CTAF at non-towered airports, covering topics like airspeeds, pitch attitudes, visual references, flight control inputs, and engine parameters. This approach helps maintain a sterile flight deck and supports situational awareness.
When teaching takeoffs, instructors should create realistic scenarios without introducing unnecessary hazards, ensuring the scenarios mimic real-world conditions learners will encounter. For instance, specifying realistic points for imaginary obstacles during confined or obstacle clearance takeoffs, and closely monitoring pitch attitude and airspeed during soft-field takeoffs, are crucial. Instructors should also ensure sufficient spacing from other aircraft to avoid hazards like wake turbulence and insufficient separation. This not only enhances safety but also teaches sound decision-making and risk management skills.
Landings:
Flight instructors should focus on real-time conditions and solutions during landings rather than teaching mechanically, to help learners manage changing conditions and maintain stable approaches. Instructors should guide learners in choosing appropriate aiming and touchdown points based on aircraft type, landing type, environment, and conditions. They should avoid excessive coaching on final approach to maintain attention to collision avoidance and situational awareness. Instructors must be skilled in handling unique landing conditions, such as strong winds or short fields, and remain aware of potential learner errors, providing concise prompts to ensure safe and effective landings.

Aeronautical Decision-Making (ADM) to include using Crew Resource Management (CRM) or Single-Pilot Resource Management (SRM), as appropriate (AIH 1-15)

Explain how Aeronautical Decision-Making (ADM) integrates with Crew Resource Management (CRM) in multi-crew environments. What are the key principles of each?
Key Principles:
CRM focuses on using all available resources (human resources, hardware, and information) effectively. Key principles include communication, situational awareness, problem solving, decision making, and teamwork.
Integration with ADM:
In CRM, ADM is used to foster a collaborative decision-making process where all crew members contribute their expertise and insights to manage the flight safely and efficiently. Effective CRM ensures that decisions are not made in isolation but are the result of a collective assessment of the situation by the crew.
Contribution to Safety and Efficiency:
CRM enhances safety by pooling the skills and experiences of all crew members, thereby reducing the likelihood of errors. It also improves efficiency by ensuring that all crew members are actively engaged in the operational process and decision-making, leading to more informed and optimal choices.

Explain how Aeronautical Decision-Making (ADM) integrates with Single-Pilot Resource Management (SRM) in solo flight operations. What are the key principles of each?
Key Principles
SRM involves the adaptation of CRM principles to the unique challenges of flying solo. It includes task management, risk management, situational awareness, and decision-making skills.
Integration with ADM:
In SRM, ADM becomes a solitary practice where the single pilot must use self-discipline to consult available resources such as ATC, weather data, and navigational aids to make informed decisions. The pilot must manage workload, maintain situational awareness, and make decisions independently.
Contribution to Safety and Efficiency:
SRM is critical in helping a solo pilot manage all aspects of flight by ensuring that they do not become overwhelmed, maintaining an awareness of their environment, and using all available resources to mitigate risks.

What is the 5-Ps checklist?
The Plan:
The plan encompasses the mission or task, including elements like cross-country planning, weather, route, fuel, and currency of publications. It should be regularly reviewed and updated throughout the flight to adapt to changes such as delays, weather, or temporary flight restrictions. The plan goes beyond the flight plan to include all events surrounding the flight, emphasizing that it is dynamic and subject to change.
The Plane:
This refers to the mechanical and cosmetic condition of the aircraft, including advanced avionics, database currency, automation status, and emergency backup systems. Factors like pilot proficiency, currency, and fatigue also play a role in assessing the plane's readiness for flight.
The Pilot:
Pilots must consider their own physical and mental state, especially when flying at high altitudes, over long distances, or in challenging weather. Fatigue and prolonged flight can impair judgment and decision-making. The 5P process helps pilots recognize their physiological condition before and during the flight to make safer decisions.
The Passengers:
Passenger interactions can influence pilot decision-making, especially when they have pressing commitments. Pilots should consider passengers' needs and preferences, involving them in decisions and managing their expectations. This can reduce pressure on the pilot and lead to safer outcomes. Understanding the risk tolerance of non-pilot passengers is also important.
The Programming:
Modern electronic displays, GPS, and autopilots can reduce workload but also capture a pilot's attention. Pilots should plan when and where to program these devices and be familiar with the equipment. Handling last-minute changes and reprogramming while flying requires careful consideration of personal capabilities and equipment familiarity.
The SRM process involves regularly reviewing the Plan, Plane, Pilot, Passengers, and Programming before and during the flight to make informed decisions based on the current situation, ensuring all risk factors are considered.

How can a Certified Flight Instructor (CFI) utilize scenario-based training (SBT) to demonstrate, teach, and assess risk management and Aeronautical Decision-Making (ADM) skills within the context of a Task specified by the evaluator?
Develop scenarios that replicate real-world flight situations, incorporating factors such as weather, aircraft performance, and airspace considerations. Present scenarios that require the student to identify hazards, assess risks, and make decisions based on available information. Encourage the student to verbalize their thought process and decision-making rationale throughout the scenario. Provide guidance and feedback on the student's risk assessment and decision-making skills, emphasizing the importance of prioritizing safety. Use debriefing sessions to review the scenario, discuss lessons learned, and reinforce key principles of risk management and ADM. Repeat the process with different scenarios to reinforce learning and develop proficiency in risk management and ADM skills.

How does a Certified Flight Instructor (CFI) identify, assess, and mitigate risks commonly associated with flight instruction?
Maintain situational awareness throughout the instructional process. Regularly assess environmental factors such as weather, airspace congestion, and airport conditions. Evaluate the student's proficiency, readiness, and physiological state before each flight. Implement appropriate risk mitigation strategies, such as adjusting lesson plans, modifying flight profiles, or rescheduling flights if necessary. Continuously communicate with students about safety concerns and reinforce a safety-first mentality. Stay updated on current regulations, best practices, and industry trends to inform risk management decisions.

How does a Certified Flight Instructor (CFI) demonstrate awareness and oversight of the learner's actions, ensuring timely and appropriate supervision, intervention, or mitigation as needed, according to the Aviation Instructor's Handbook?
Maintain continuous awareness of the learner's actions and progress during flight training. Employ effective observation techniques to monitor student performance and decision-making. Intervene promptly and appropriately to correct errors or deviations from standards. Provide clear and concise instructions or guidance to redirect the learner as needed. Utilize positive reinforcement and constructive feedback to reinforce desired behaviors. Adjust instructional techniques or lesson plans to address specific areas of concern or difficulty for the learner. Ensure a safe and supportive learning environment conducive to effective instruction and learning outcomes.

How does a Certified Flight Instructor (CFI) demonstrate awareness of the learner's cognitive/physiological state, and what timely actions can be taken to mitigate anxiety, fatigue, or other obstructions to learning, as outlined in the Aviation Instructor's Handbook?
Monitor the learner's demeanor, behavior, and communication during training sessions.Recognize signs of anxiety, fatigue, or other physiological states that may impact learning. Engage in open communication with the learner to address any concerns or challenges they may be experiencing. Adjust the pace and intensity of instruction to accommodate the learner's needs and ensure optimal learning conditions. Encourage breaks or rest periods as needed to alleviate fatigue and maintain focus. Provide reassurance and support to help alleviate anxiety and foster a positive learning environment. Utilize relaxation techniques or stress management strategies to help the learner cope with any obstacles to learning.

How does a Certified Flight Instructor (CFI) maintain overall situational awareness of the aircraft's dynamic state, its position in space, and vigilance for unexpected events or changing circumstances in the environment, according to the Aviation Instructor's Handbook?
Regularly scan instruments, outside references, and airspace to maintain awareness of the aircraft's dynamic state and position. Stay vigilant for changes in weather, traffic, and other environmental factors that could affect flight safety. Utilize effective communication with air traffic control (ATC) and other pilots to enhance situational awareness. Encourage the development of effective scanning techniques and the use of checklists to ensure comprehensive awareness. Foster a proactive mindset that anticipates and plans for potential challenges or unexpected events during flight.

How does a Certified Flight Instructor (CFI) effectively model and teach safety practices?
Demonstrate strict adherence to safety protocols and procedures during all aspects of flight instruction. Emphasize the importance of safety as a top priority in every aspect of aviation training. Set a positive example by consistently following regulatory requirements and industry best practices. Encourage open communication about safety concerns and foster a safety-oriented culture within the learning environment. Incorporate safety briefings into preflight and post-flight discussions to highlight key safety considerations. Provide constructive feedback and guidance to students to help them develop safe operating habits and decision-making skills. Continuously reinforce the importance of safety through classroom instruction, flight demonstrations, and debriefing sessions.

How can an aviation instructor effectively balance the task of providing instruction while ensuring collision avoidance during flight training?
Maintain constant situational awareness by scanning for potential hazards. Use clear and concise communication to provide instructions well in advance. Prioritize safety above all else, taking immediate action to avoid collisions if necessary. Utilize simulation and practice to build skills in anticipating and avoiding potential conflicts.Establish a feedback loop for learners to ask questions and clarify instructions without compromising safety. Consider the use of technology such as collision avoidance systems to aid in safety management. Start with simple scenarios and gradually increase complexity to develop proficiency without overwhelming learners. Maintain a calm demeanor to effectively manage both tasks in high-pressure situations.

How can aviation instructors minimize unnecessary distractions during flight training sessions?
Establish a structured lesson plan with clear objectives to maintain focus. Limit non-essential communication during critical phases of flight. Encourage students to prioritize tasks and manage time effectively. Minimize interruptions from external sources such as radio chatter or passenger conversations. Set expectations for electronic device usage and encourage their use only for relevant training purposes. Model focused behavior by maintaining attention on the task at hand. Provide guidance on managing stress and staying composed in distracting environments. Regularly debrief to identify and address any distractions encountered during training sessions.

What is coordinated flight, and why is it important for student pilots to understand this concept?
Coordinated flight refers to an aircraft's flight condition where the forces of lift, weight, thrust, and drag are balanced, resulting in straight and level flight without slipping or skidding. It's crucial for student pilots to understand coordinated flight because it ensures efficient and safe operation of the aircraft. Understanding coordinated flight helps maintain control, stability, and performance, particularly during maneuvers such as turns, climbs, and descents. Failure to maintain coordinated flight can lead to adverse effects such as increased drag, loss of lift, and decreased control authority, increasing the risk of stalls or spins.

How should instructors promote awareness of control manipulation among flight crew members, particularly through the positive exchange of flight controls?
Instructors should establish a clear protocol for the positive exchange of flight controls before initiating flight maneuvers. Encourage verbal communication between pilots to confirm who is manipulating the controls at any given time. Emphasize the importance of maintaining situational awareness by both pilots, even when not actively manipulating the controls. Teach the concept of positive transfer of control, where one pilot verbally confirms the transfer before physically relinquishing or assuming control. Practice positive exchange procedures during various flight phases to ensure both pilots are proficient in the process. Reinforce the idea that the pilot flying should always confirm control inputs and intentions with the pilot not flying, promoting a shared mental model of the aircraft's status.

Why is it essential for pilots to maintain continuous awareness of the aircraft's dynamic state and position in the National Airspace System (NAS)?
Continuous awareness of the aircraft's dynamic state and position is crucial for maintaining situational awareness and safety during flight operations. It enables pilots to anticipate and react to changes in the environment, including traffic, weather, and terrain. Awareness of the aircraft's position within the National Airspace System (NAS) helps pilots comply with airspace regulations and avoid airspace violations. Understanding the dynamic state of the aircraft, such as airspeed, altitude, and heading, allows pilots to make timely adjustments to maintain control and stability. Awareness of the NAS helps pilots navigate efficiently, communicate effectively with air traffic control, and avoid conflicts with other aircraft.
