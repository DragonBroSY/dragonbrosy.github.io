Multi-Engine Study Guide

Piper Seminole checkride prep. Start with the KDKX checkride guide, drill the flashcards, then listen to the full training supplement chapter by chapter or read the transcript.

Checkride Prep · Gary Sumner, KDKX

Updated November 16, 2026

How Gary runs the ride

Gary grades proficiency, not perfection. All three reports agree that he wants small deviations caught, spoken out loud and corrected right away. Above everything else, he grades how smoothly you move the throttles and mixtures.

This guide is for KDKX (Knoxville Downtown Island), where every report flies the RNAV 26 into DKX. The technical content comes only from FAA publications: AFH FAA-H-8083-3C Ch. 13, FAA-S-ACS-7B and 14 CFR. Seminole numbers come from your FAA-approved PA-44 POH/AFM, so check them against your copy.

  • Check-in: pilot certificate, medical, government photo ID, logbook with endorsements, IACRA application and aircraft documents. He prefers paper copies, organized and tabbed.
  • Oral: a conversation built on a scenario, usually a cross-country with passengers or a high-density-altitude trip such as KDKX to KSAF or ABQ. He wants you to explain why, not recite definitions.
  • Flight: he stays quiet when things are going well. If he starts talking, something needs fixing. Brief each maneuver, and ask for time if you need it.
  • Possible outcomes: a temporary certificate, a notice of disapproval or a letter of discontinuance.
  • Before the ride: study the KDKX airport diagram, including the run-up areas and the taxi routes to Runways 8 and 26. Also check the Class C shelf to the west on the sectional; one report says it starts at 2,500 ft.
Oral: certificates, documents and currency

The currency question on every ride comes down to one rule. Passenger currency goes by category and class, while instrument currency goes by category only. That means single-engine instrument currency carries over to the Seminole, but single-engine landings do not count toward multiengine passenger currency.

Aircraft documents (ARROW): airworthiness certificate, registration, radio station license (needed only for international flights), operating limitations (AFM/POH, placards and markings), weight and balance. References: 91.203, 91.9, POH/AFM.

Inspections (AV1ATES): annual (91.409(a)), VOR check within 30 days for IFR (91.171), 100-hour when carrying people for hire or giving flight instruction for hire (91.409(b)), altimeter and static system within 24 calendar months for IFR in controlled airspace (91.411), transponder within 24 calendar months (91.413), ELT every 12 calendar months plus battery rules (91.207), and the status of ADs (91.403, 91.417).

Inoperative equipment: the Seminole has no MEL under Part 91, so use 91.213(d). The item must not be required by the VFR-day type certificate, 91.205, the KOEL/equipment list or an AD. It must be removed or deactivated and placarded INOPERATIVE, and the pilot must decide it is not a hazard.

If asked whether a twin is inherently safer: not necessarily. The AFH says the second engine gives you options only if you are proficient and the airplane has the performance for the conditions. Under 14 CFR part 23, a light twin weighing 6,000 lb or less with VSO of 61 kt or less does not have to show any positive single-engine climb at all. The airplane's performance and your skill decide the outcome (AFH Ch. 13, Performance and Limitations).

Oral: critical engine, Vmc and SMACFUM

VMC (red radial line) is the minimum speed at which you can keep directional control with the critical engine inoperative. It says nothing about whether the airplane can climb. Gary wants you to explain how each factor moves VMC, not just list the acronym.

Critical engine (PAST). On a conventional twin where both propellers turn clockwise from the cockpit, losing the left engine is worse. At high angle of attack, the descending blade of each propeller makes more thrust (P-factor). The right engine's descending blade sits farther from the centerline, so it has a longer moment arm.

  • P-factor: the right engine's thrust line is farther from the centerline, so yaw is worse when the left engine fails.
  • Accelerated slipstream: the right engine's stronger slipstream sits farther outboard, so roll is worse when the left engine fails.
  • Spiraling slipstream: on a conventional twin, the left engine's slipstream hits the vertical tail and helps counter yaw. Lose the left engine and that help goes away.
  • Torque: the right engine's torque rolls the airplane left, toward the dead left engine.
  • Seminole: the propellers counter-rotate (the right one turns counterclockwise), so either engine failing gives the same asymmetry. There is no critical engine (AFH Ch. 13).

How VMC is set (14 CFR part 23, as the AFH describes it). One report cited 25.149. The Seminole is certificated under part 23, and the AFH cites 23.149.

VMC versus stall speed. As altitude goes up, VMC drops but indicated stall speed stays the same. At some altitude the airplane will stall before you lose directional control. A stall with asymmetric power can lead to a spin, and the AFH calls twins' spin recovery generally very poor. So recover at the first sign of either.

Zero sideslip (best single-engine climb). Bank about 1.5 to 2.5 degrees toward the operating engine, with the ball one-third to one-half out toward the operating engine: raise the dead. Then the rudder and the bank share the work of controlling yaw.

Blue line (VYSE) gives the best single-engine rate of climb, or the least sink rate above the single-engine absolute ceiling. VXSE gives the best single-engine angle of climb. VSSE is the slowest speed at which an engine may be failed on purpose; if none is published, use VYSE.

Oral: performance and weight and balance

Bring finished performance figures for the actual day: a short-field takeoff over a 50 ft obstacle, accelerate-stop, accelerate-go, single-engine service and absolute ceilings, single-engine climb at field elevation, and weight and balance with the envelope plotted. Gary asked to see all of them on one ride and walked through the numbers.

TermFAA definition (AFH Ch. 13)
Accelerate-stop distanceRunway needed to accelerate to VR or VLOF, lose an engine, then bring the airplane to a complete stop
Accelerate-go distanceDistance to accelerate to VR or VLOF, lose an engine, then continue the takeoff and climb to 50 ft
Single-engine service ceilingAltitude where the single-engine climb rate drops to 50 fpm
Single-engine absolute ceilingAltitude where the single-engine climb rate drops to zero
Climb gradientAltitude gained per 100 ft of horizontal travel; 1.5 ft per 100 ft is 1.5%

Why accelerate-go in a Seminole is a poor bet. Part 23 does not require a twin this size to climb on one engine at all. The AFH's guidance is that a failure before the gear comes up means landing on the remaining runway or straight ahead. Once the gear is up, continue only if climb performance exists. Single-engine climb at a heavy weight on a hot day may be zero or negative, and the accelerate-go chart can run far past the runway you have.

High-density-altitude scenario (KDKX to KSAF or ABQ). Work through these points out loud:

  1. Higher density altitude means less power, less propeller efficiency and a longer takeoff roll, and a normally aspirated engine loses climb performance with both engines and on one.
  2. If the single-engine service ceiling is below the field elevation, an engine failure after takeoff means you cannot hold altitude. You are committed to an off-airport landing or the remaining runway.
  3. Ways to reduce the risk: lighter weight, a cooler time of day, a longer runway, a departure path clear of terrain, and a briefed plan for an engine failure.
  4. VMC goes down as density altitude goes up, but stall speed does not, which raises the stall/spin risk (see the VMC section).

Drift-down. Above the single-engine ceiling after a failure, fly VYSE to keep the rate of altitude loss as low as possible. You sink fastest right after the failure, and the sink rate eases as you approach the single-engine ceiling (AFH Ch. 13). Plan a route and altitude that clear terrain at that ceiling.

Weight and balance (Aircraft Weight and Balance Handbook, FAA-H-8083-1). CG = total moment ÷ total weight; plot the result in the weight-versus-CG envelope. To check a passenger move, use ΔCG = (weight moved × distance moved) ÷ total weight. Moving a heavy passenger to the rear seats puts the CG aft. That gives the rudder a shorter arm (higher VMC), less longitudinal stability, lighter pitch forces and harder stall recovery. It also shortens the elevator arm, which helps cruise speed slightly.

Oral: Seminole systems

Gary asks how each system behaves when something fails, and where the parts are on the airplane. Be ready to touch them during preflight. The general principles below come from the AFH. Seminole quantities and limits come from your POH, and you should fill them in yourself rather than trust a study guide.

Propellers: constant-speed and full-feathering (AFH Ch. 13)

  • Governor oil pressure moves the blades toward low pitch and high RPM.
  • The nitrogen charge in the hub, the large internal spring and the blade counterweights all push toward high pitch and feather.
  • If oil pressure is lost in flight, the propeller goes toward feather. This is fail-safe, and it is why an engine with an oil leak can end up feathered.
  • Anti-feathering (centrifugal stop) pins engage below about 800 RPM. They keep the propeller from feathering on a normal ground shutdown, so feather before RPM drops below that.
  • Feathering can take up to 10 seconds.
  • The unfeathering accumulator stores oil under pressure. Moving the prop lever forward releases that oil to move the blades out of feather for an airstart, without relying on the starter alone.
  • A windmilling propeller at low pitch can make as much drag as the airframe's entire parasite drag. That is why identify, verify and feather comes so early in the flow.

Fuel

  • Know the flow path: tank, selector, strainer/filter, electric fuel pump, engine-driven pump, then to the carburetor, with the primer solenoid branching off. Know where the fuel pressure gauge reads.
  • Crossfeed (AFH): mainly a one-engine-inoperative procedure to extend range by feeding the good engine from the opposite tank. Never use it for takeoff, or for a normal landing with both engines running. Check it on the ground per the POH.
  • From the POH: total and usable fuel, selector positions (ON / OFF / X-FEED) and crossfeed limitations.

Landing gear (from the POH, know these cold)

  • Electrically powered hydraulic pump: where the pump and reservoir are, and what holds the gear up and what locks it down.
  • The squat switch that prevents retraction on the ground, which explains why the gear stays down if the selector is bumped up while taxiing.
  • Emergency extension: selector position, procedure and airspeed limit.
  • Indications: three green, the in-transit light, and the gear warning horn logic.

Electrical

  • Know the alternator output, the bus voltage, the battery and where the external power receptacle is (POH). Know the jump-start procedure and which engine to start first.
  • The engines keep running after a battery or alternator failure because the magnetos generate their own ignition and do not need the electrical system (PHAK, Ch. 7).

Engine instruments: normal ranges for oil pressure, oil temperature, fuel pressure and CHT/EGT, where the sensors are, and what a reading means during an engine failure.

Flight: expected sequence and ACS tolerances

The three reports describe nearly the same profile. Expect simulated engine failures in four places: on the takeoff roll, during the climb, as a planned shutdown and on the instrument approach.

  1. Preflight by checklist (he may watch from the FBO). Taxi slowly, at about a brisk walking pace.
  2. Normal takeoff, pattern and landing. Taxi back for Runway 8.
  3. Rejected takeoff: he applies rudder or pulls a mixture early in the roll. Then the takeoff continues.
  4. Climb toward the northeast practice area. A simulated engine failure comes around 3,000 ft MSL.
  5. Steep turns left and right.
  6. Slow flight, power-off stall, power-on stall and accelerated stall. The order varies, and on one ride he called for a recovery right after slow flight.
  7. VMC demonstration.
  8. Engine shutdown for an oil leak (Mayday, feather, secure), then an airstart.
  9. Pop-up IFR: RNAV 26 into DKX with one engine inoperative, then circle to Runway 8.
  10. Short-field takeoff and landing with a touchdown target at the third stripe. Taxi back by checklist.

ACS tolerances, Commercial AMEL (FAA-S-ACS-7B). The additional-rating table for adding AMEL to a commercial ASEL certificate requires all of Area VII (slow flight and stalls) and all of Area X (multiengine operations). It also requires IV.A, B, E and F, V.A steep turns, and IX.E, F and G engine failures. X.C and X.D cover the instrument tasks for multiengine instrument privileges, and every report flew them.

The ACS also limits simulated failures: none in the takeoff roll above 50% of VMC, none after liftoff below 400 ft AGL or below VSSE/VYSE, and actual feathering only at or above 3,000 ft AGL.

Flight: engine failure flows

Every engine failure starts with directional control, then airspeed, then the flow. Say each step out loud. Gary grades the order and whether you verify before you feather.

Rejected takeoff (IX.E). Expect it early in the roll, as rudder pressure from Gary or a pulled mixture.

  1. Bring both throttles to idle at once. The ACS wants this smooth and prompt, and one report notes Gary expects power off first, not after you regain the centerline.
  2. Keep directional control with rudder, nosewheel steering and brakes.
  3. Call ABORT. Stopping in the shortest possible distance is not always the goal; a controlled stop beats a swerve (AFH).

Engine failure in climb or cruise (X.A, IX.F). The AFH sequence is:

  1. Control: rudder to stop the yaw, and pitch for VYSE (blue line).
  2. Power up: mixtures, then props, then throttles forward, all smoothly.
  3. Clean up: flaps up, gear up.
  4. Identify: dead foot, dead engine. Identify by the control input you need, not by the gauges.
  5. Verify: slowly retard the throttle you suspect. If performance does not change, you have the right engine.
  6. Feather: pull that propeller lever fully aft. Bank about 2 degrees toward the good engine with the ball about half out (raise the dead), and trim.
  7. Secure and declare: run the securing checklist (mixture, mags, alternator, fuel pump, selector, cowl flaps), declare an emergency, and pick the nearest suitable airport.

If retarding the throttle does cost performance, the engine is still giving partial power. Keep it running until you have a safe altitude and airspeed (AFH). Below 3,000 ft AGL, the ACS calls for simulating feather by setting zero thrust. One report said out loud, "below 3,000 AGL, I would feather and cut off the mixture," and Gary accepted that.

Planned shutdown for an oil leak, then airstart (X.A).

  • When he reports oil streaking from an engine, answer: "I'll shut it down now; troubleshooting an oil leak isn't worth the risk." Then simulate a Mayday call and name the nearest airport.
  • You already know which engine it is, so the flow is short: throttle to idle, feather, mixture to cutoff, then the securing checklist. He may let you call out the securing items without doing them.
  • Airstart: follow the POH restart checklist, at or above 3,000 ft AGL. Bring power back very slowly, and let the engine warm up before adding power.
Flight: VMC demonstration and stalls

Recover at the first sign of lost directional control or a stall warning, then bring the power back slowly. Gary limits how much right rudder you can use, so you will lose directional control earlier than in training, typically around 75 to 80 kt. He wants you to say "losing directional control" and recover right away.

VMC demonstration (AFH Ch. 13, ACS X.B)

  1. Clearing turns. At least 3,000 ft AGL, gear up, flaps in the takeoff position (or as the POH specifies).
  2. Slow to about 10 kt above VSSE or VYSE. Bring the critical engine (simulated) to idle and the other to takeoff power.
  3. Hold the entry heading with rudder, with up to 5 degrees of bank toward the operating engine.
  4. Raise the pitch to slow about 1 kt per second.
  5. At the first sign of lost directional control, the stall warning or buffet: reduce power on the operating engine to stop the yaw, and lower the nose to gain speed at the same time.
  6. Recover to straight flight within 20 degrees of the entry heading at VSSE or VYSE ±5 kt. Then smoothly add power on the operating engine and show controlled flight before restoring symmetric power.

Never let it stall with asymmetric power, because a spin is likely and twins recover from spins poorly (AFH).

Stalls (ACS VII.B-D, AFH multiengine stall guidance)

  • Start at least 3,000 ft AGL in a multiengine airplane, with clearing turns first.
  • Recover in this order: reduce angle of attack first, then level the wings, then add power as needed, smoothly. Do not jam the throttles forward.
  • Power-off stall: approach or landing configuration. Power-on stall: takeoff configuration, at least 65% power. Accelerated stall: 45 degrees of bank, at or below VA.
  • The AFH prohibits single-engine stalls and simulated engine failures during stall entry or recovery.
  • Do not assume the order. One report had Gary call for a recovery from slow flight before the power-on stall; another went straight from slow flight into the power-off stall.

Steep turns: 50 degrees of bank, clearing turns first, and fix altitude errors right away. He accepts small deviations caught quickly, but not a large altitude loss.

Flight: single-engine RNAV 26, circle to 8, and short-field

On the single-engine approach, keep the gear and flaps up until you are abeam the numbers on the circle. With the gear down you may not be able to hold altitude on one engine. Two of the three reports call this the trap on this ride.

Single-engine RNAV 26 into DKX (ACS X.D, AFH engine-inoperative approach)

  1. Gary arranges a pop-up clearance. Expect vectors to SPROC, the IAF. Brief the approach early and load it in the 430.
  2. The engine failure usually comes on the intermediate leg or near the FAF. Run the full flow and keep flying the lateral path. Fly the approach at VYSE.
  3. A few miles before the FAF, do the before-landing items except gear and flaps: mixtures and props forward, fuel pump on, and say "gear and flaps stay up for the single-engine approach." Then crossing the FAF only takes a small power reduction to descend.
  4. Gary has used traffic pattern altitude as the circling MDA. About 2 NM before the MAP, he had the pilot break left onto downwind for Runway 8.
  5. Abeam the numbers: gear down, confirm three green. The AFH says to extend the gear on downwind abeam the intended landing point.
  6. Add flaps only when the landing is assured. The AFH allows delaying full flaps until short final, and one report used 25 degrees abeam the numbers. Stay at VYSE until short final with the landing assured, and never slower than VMC.
  7. Staying a little high on the circle lets you reduce to idle in the flare without dragging it in on one engine. Avoid big power reductions right after passing obstacles (AFH).
  8. Gary has said both throttles are available if you need a go-around.

Short-field takeoff (IV.E). Clear the approach on the 430 and OBS the runway before departure. One report flew the POH obstacle-clearance speed (82 KIAS in the student's notes) until clear of the trees, then gear up and VY (88 KIAS); check both against your POH. He may combine this takeoff with the rejected-takeoff scenario.

Short-field landing (IV.F). The target is usually the third runway stripe, and the ACS tolerance is 0 to +100 ft. Fly the POH approach speed ±5 kt. One report used about 80 kt on short final and 75 over the numbers, then idle. He sometimes records the landing. He has little tolerance for landing long, but he does not want you forcing the airplane onto the spot either; go around instead. On a wet runway he had the pilot use normal braking and simulate maximum braking.

Gary's emphasis and your final checklist

Move every throttle, mixture and prop lever slowly, more slowly than you think you need to. All three reports name this as his top item; on one ride he said inputs that fast could flip a King Air. The AFH also recommends a smooth throttle reduction for any simulated failure below 3,000 ft AGL.

What he watches

  • Smooth power on stall recoveries, the VMC demonstration recovery and the airstart
  • Verifying the dead engine before feathering, with every step spoken
  • A traffic scan between maneuvers: look outside, then check the MFD and your iPad
  • Declaring an emergency on every simulated or prompted engine failure
  • Using the checklist every time and staying ahead of the airplane
  • Catching and fixing deviations right away
